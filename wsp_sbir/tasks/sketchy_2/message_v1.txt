TASK. You are given the weights of CLIP ViT-B/32 (OpenAI's original release). Its image encoder is a vision transformer: a 32x32-stride convolution cuts the 224x224 image into 7x7 = 49 patch tokens, a class token is prepended, positional embeddings are added, then LayerNorm (ln_pre), 12 pre-LayerNorm residual blocks of width 768 (12 attention heads of size 64; an MLP of width 3,072 with QuickGELU), LayerNorm (ln_post) on the class token and a 768 -> 512 projection (proj). The model is used for zero-shot sketch-based image retrieval: the same image encoder embeds a hand-drawn sketch (the query) and photos (the gallery), the photos are ranked by cosine similarity to the sketch, and a photo is relevant if it shows the same object category as the sketch. Your job is to make this retrieval better on object categories the model has not been adapted to, by EDITING THE IMAGE ENCODER'S WEIGHTS DIRECTLY: find out what goes wrong inside the network when a sketch ranks photos of other categories above photos of its own, and repair that with targeted changes.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited encoder and the unedited one are evaluated on a zero-shot split of the Sketchy dataset: ${n_unseen} object categories, none of which is among your practice categories (no category, sketch or photo of the evaluation is in /practice), about 12,700 sketch queries against a gallery of about 12,550 photos of those categories. The protocol is fixed: every image is resized to 224x224 (no crop), converted to RGB and normalized with CLIP's mean and standard deviation; the encoder runs in float16 (your float32 edits are cast to each tensor's storage type, float16 for most matrices); the embedding is the encoder output, L2-normalized; photos are ranked by cosine similarity; the scores are mAP@200 (the main score) and P@200 (the share of relevant photos among the top 200), averaged over all sketch queries. Only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the evaluation categories.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: find out what goes wrong when retrieval fails (for example, sketch and photo embeddings of one category lying far apart because the network encodes "this is a line drawing" more strongly than "what object this is", or sketches being matched by stroke style, background or pose rather than by object), identify the components responsible (specific neurons, heads, directions or circuits) and change them in a way you can justify with evidence. The same weights embed sketches and photos, so every edit acts on both. Out of scope, and refused by save_edit: the text encoder and logit_scale (they are not used by the retrieval; only tensors whose names start with "visual." can be edited), and any edit that only multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Edits that do not depend on what you found (adding noise, rescaling whole layers) are not what we are looking for. Because the evaluation uses new categories, edits should repair a general mechanism, not memorize the practice categories.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Diagnose first. Look at how retrieval fails on practice categories: which categories and sketches fail, what the wrongly top-ranked photos have in common, how far a sketch's embedding is from the photos of its own category compared with those of other categories, and what separates sketch embeddings from photo embeddings regardless of category. Go after the largest failure you can explain.
2. Localize causally. That a component differs between sketches and photos, or between good and bad queries, is not enough. Before relying on a component, show that changing it changes the behaviour you predicted, and try several strengths to see a dose-response.
3. Measure with enough power, on the right unit. Queries of one category are strongly correlated, and the evaluation is on new categories, so the standard error over categories is the one that matters for carrying over; compare original and edited weights on the SAME images (a retrieval pass with "model": "both" does this) and do not compare with a score measured on other images or another gallery (mAP depends on the gallery). Keep an edit only if its gain is clearly larger than its standard error on categories that were not used to choose it. Read passes are cheap and dense: one pass gives the activations of up to 1,024 images. Edits that change a tensor by a fraction of a percent usually have effects too small to see, and float16 rounding erases the smallest ones; test strengths large enough to matter and back off if they hurt.
4. Plan your forward passes (see the budget above): for example one diagnostic retrieval with activations, read passes for dense measurements, and a final paired retrieval ("model": "both") on held-out categories to confirm.
