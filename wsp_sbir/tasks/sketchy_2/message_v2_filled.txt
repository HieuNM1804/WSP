TASK. You are given the weights of CLIP ViT-B/32 (OpenAI's original release). We use it for ZERO-SHOT SKETCH-BASED IMAGE RETRIEVAL (ZS-SBIR): a hand-drawn sketch is the query, a set of photos is the gallery, the same image encoder embeds both, photos are ranked by cosine similarity to the sketch, and a photo is relevant if it shows the same object category as the sketch. This is a cross-domain retrieval problem (query and gallery come from two different visual domains) and a downstream task CLIP was never trained for. Your job is to make this retrieval better on object categories the model has never been adapted to, by EDITING THE IMAGE ENCODER'S WEIGHTS DIRECTLY, with no training: find out what goes wrong inside the network when a sketch ranks photos of other categories above photos of its own category, identify the components responsible, and repair them with targeted, justified changes to the weights.

BACKGROUND: THE MODEL. The image encoder is a vision transformer. A convolution with a 32x32 kernel and stride 32 (visual.conv1, no bias) cuts the normalized 224x224 RGB image into 7x7 = 49 patch tokens of width 768; a learned class token (visual.class_embedding) is prepended; learned positional embeddings (visual.positional_embedding, 50 x 768) are added; LayerNorm (visual.ln_pre); then 12 pre-LayerNorm residual blocks (visual.transformer.resblocks.0 to .11), each x = x + attention(ln_1(x)) followed by x = x + MLP(ln_2(x)), with 12 attention heads of size 64 and an MLP of width 3,072 with QuickGELU; finally LayerNorm (visual.ln_post) on the class token only and a 768 -> 512 linear projection without bias (visual.proj). The embedding used for retrieval is that 512-dimensional output, L2-normalized. The exact layout of every weight (for example the row order of the attention matrices: query, key and value rows stacked, each split into 12 heads of 64) is in /model/arch.json. CLIP was trained with a contrastive loss on hundreds of millions of web image-caption pairs: its image embedding is shaped to match the text of a caption, not to match a sketch with a photo of the same object. It is usually strong at recognizing objects in photos, and it also recognizes many sketches, but its embedding of an image mixes what the object is with how the image looks (drawing or photograph, color, texture, background, style), and nothing in its training asked a sketch and a photo of the same object to land close together. The text encoder is part of the release but plays no role in this retrieval.

BACKGROUND: THE CROSS-DOMAIN PROBLEM. Sketches and photos differ in almost everything except the object. Here a sketch is thin black strokes on a white background (in these files about 95% of the pixels of a typical sketch are white), with no color, no texture, no background scene, simplified or exaggerated shapes, missing parts and an amateur's proportions. A photo is a natural image with color, texture, lighting, clutter and background, and its object can be small, partial or occluded. A good embedding for sketch-based retrieval must be DOMAIN-INVARIANT (a sketch and a photo of the same category land close together, whatever the drawing style or the photo's background) while staying CATEGORY-DISCRIMINATIVE (different categories stay apart), and both properties must hold on categories it has never been adapted to. Typical ways retrieval fails: every sketch is closer to every other sketch than to any photo, so the "drawing versus photograph" information dominates the geometry; a sketch ranks photos by low-level appearance (a white or empty background, simple outlines, a dominant shape or orientation) instead of by object; a sketch is matched to a visually similar but different category; a few photos are near the top for many unrelated sketches (hubs); some categories are hard because the sketch is ambiguous. Methods published for this task usually train something on the seen categories (prompt vectors, adapters, the encoder itself, with triplet or distillation losses) and then apply it to unseen categories; what they learn has to remove domain information and keep category information in a way that carries over. You may not train anything: you must find where in the network the problem arises and change the weights so that it does not, based on what you understand.

BACKGROUND: WHAT ZERO-SHOT MEANS HERE. The evaluation categories are disjoint from every category you can see. An edit that helps only the practice categories (for example by encoding their particular shapes, or by directions that are specific to a few categories) will not help the evaluation and may hurt it. An edit that removes a general cause of cross-domain mismatch (something every sketch shares, or something that makes photos rank by appearance) should carry over. Treat the practice categories the way the evaluation treats its categories: choose edits on some categories and measure them on others that were never used to choose them.

THE DATA. Practice data (none of it is in the evaluation): 104 Sketchy categories with 57,587 sketches (468 to 693 per category) and 72,945 photos (554 to 800 per category), stored as 224x224 RGB files: /practice/sketch/<category>/<file>.png and /practice/photo/<category>/<file>.jpg. /practice/index.jsonl has one JSON object per image with id (the path under /practice, for example "sketch/airplane/n02691156_10151-1.png"), modality ("sketch" or "photo") and category; /practice/categories.json lists the categories. About 100 photos per category (10,399 in all) are the photos the sketches were drawn from, usually 4 to 8 sketches each: a sketch's file name is its photo's name, a dash and a number (photo n02691156_10151.jpg, sketches n02691156_10151-1.png, n02691156_10151-2.png, ...). These matched pairs show the same object instance in the two domains, so differences within a pair isolate the domain from the category and the instance. The other photos (ext_<number>.jpg and the remaining n<...>.jpg) have no sketch.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited encoder and the unedited one are evaluated on a zero-shot split of the Sketchy dataset: 21 object categories, none of which is among your practice categories (no category, sketch or photo of the evaluation is in /practice), about 12,700 sketch queries against a gallery of about 12,550 photos of those categories (about 600 photos per category, so every sketch must find the photos of its category among those of the other categories). The protocol is fixed: every image is resized to 224x224 (no crop), converted to RGB and normalized with CLIP's mean and standard deviation; the encoder runs in float16 (your float32 edits are cast to each tensor's storage type, float16 for most matrices, float32 for LayerNorm parameters and embeddings: see DTYPES); the embedding is the encoder output, L2-normalized; every sketch is a query against the whole gallery, ranked by cosine similarity. The main score is mAP@200: for each sketch, among its top 200 ranked photos, the precision at the position of each relevant photo, averaged over the relevant photos found in the top 200 (a sketch with no relevant photo in its top 200 scores 0), then averaged over all sketches. The second score is P@200: the share of relevant photos among the top 200, averaged over all sketches. mAP@200 therefore rewards putting relevant photos at the very top of the ranking; P@200 rewards filling the top 200 with relevant photos. Only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the evaluation categories, and their scores are never shown to you.

WHAT YOU SHOULD FIND OUT. Use the practice data to answer these questions with measurements, roughly in this order, and let the answers decide your edits:
 (1) How does retrieval fail? Per category and per sketch: which categories retrieve well or badly; which categories the wrongly top-ranked photos come from and what they have in common; whether some photos are near the top for many unrelated sketches; how the similarity of a sketch to the photos of its own category compares with its similarity to other categories and to other sketches.
 (2) What separates the two domains in the embedding? For example, how much of the variance of the embeddings is explained by the domain compared with the category; whether there is a direction, or a few, along which all sketches differ from all photos; how that compares across categories (one shared offset, or category-specific); what the matched sketch-photo pairs show.
 (3) Where in the network does the domain information arise and grow? Follow it through the residual stream (the layers 0 to 12 that the tool returns), at the class token and at the patch tokens: at which depth sketches and photos become separable, at which depth category information appears, and whether the two live in the same or in different directions.
 (4) Which components write it? Single blocks, attention heads, MLP neurons, LayerNorm scales or the final projection: which of them write the domain information into the class token or into the embedding, which read it, and what they respond to in the image (for example white or empty patches, thin strokes, color or texture, the background, the position of a patch). Attention patterns can show which patches the class token reads in sketches compared with photos.
 (5) What happens when you change them? Show causally that changing a component changes the domain gap or the retrieval in the predicted way, at several strengths (dose-response), and that it does not destroy category information (for example, that photos of different categories do not collapse together).
 Then choose the edit that repairs the largest failure you can explain, confirm it on held-out categories, and stop when further changes do not clearly help.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: a mechanism you have identified (specific neurons, heads, directions or circuits, and what they do on sketches and photos) and a change of the weights that you can justify with evidence. Removing or reducing a domain-specific signal at the place where it is written, changing what a component responds to, or rebalancing a component whose effect you have measured are all in scope if you can show why they help. The same weights embed sketches and photos, so every edit acts on both domains: check its effect on both. Out of scope, and refused by save_edit: the text encoder and logit_scale (they are not used by the retrieval; only tensors whose names start with "visual." can be edited), and any edit that only multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Edits that do not depend on what you found (adding noise, rescaling whole layers) are not what we are looking for, and neither are edits aimed at particular practice categories: the evaluation uses new categories, so the repair must act on a general mechanism.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Diagnose first (questions 1 and 2 above). One retrieval pass on a diagnosis set of categories with activations gives the baseline, the failures and the per-layer features at once; analyze it thoroughly in the kernel before spending another pass. Go after the largest failure you can explain.
2. Localize causally (questions 3 to 5). That a component differs between sketches and photos, or between good and bad queries, is not enough: a component can be correlated with the domain without causing the retrieval failure. Before relying on a component, show that changing it changes the behaviour you predicted, and try several strengths to see a dose-response. Prefer the smallest change that removes the mechanism, at the place where it is written, over a large change downstream.
3. Measure with enough power, on the right unit. Queries of one category are strongly correlated and the evaluation is on new categories, so the standard error over categories is the one that matters for carrying over. Compare original and edited weights on the SAME images (a retrieval pass with "model": "both" does this) and never with a score measured on other images or another gallery. Keep an edit only if its gain is clearly larger than its standard error over categories on held-out categories that were not used to choose it. Look at both scores (mAP@200 and P@200) and at how many categories got worse, not only at the mean. Edits that change a tensor by a fraction of a percent usually have effects too small to see, and float16 rounding erases the smallest ones; test strengths large enough to matter and back off if they hurt.
4. Plan your forward passes (see the budget above; read passes count as passes too). For example: one diagnostic retrieval with activations on the diagnosis categories, read passes for dense measurements and for probe images, paired retrievals ("model": "both") to test candidate edits at a few strengths on the diagnosis categories, and a final paired retrieval on held-out categories, as large as a pass allows, to confirm the edit you submit.
