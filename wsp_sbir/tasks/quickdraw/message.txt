TASK. You are given the weights of CLIP ViT-B/32 (OpenAI's original release). We use it for ZERO-SHOT SKETCH-BASED IMAGE RETRIEVAL (ZS-SBIR): a hand-drawn sketch is the query, a set of photos is the gallery, the same image encoder embeds both, photos are ranked by cosine similarity to the sketch, and a photo is relevant if it shows the same object category as the sketch. Your goal is to make this retrieval as good as you can on object categories the model has never been adapted to, measured by mAP@all (and P@200), by EDITING THE NETWORK'S WEIGHTS DIRECTLY, with no training: find out, with measurements, what limits the retrieval inside the network, identify the components responsible, and change the weights so that the network ranks the relevant photos higher. You are free in the mechanism you go after and in the tensors you change: any tensor of the image encoder, at any depth, may be edited, alone or together with others, as long as you can show why the change helps.

BACKGROUND: THE MODEL. The image encoder is a vision transformer. A convolution with a 32x32 kernel and stride 32 (visual.conv1, no bias) cuts the normalized 224x224 RGB image into 7x7 = 49 patch tokens of width 768; a learned class token (visual.class_embedding) is prepended; learned positional embeddings (visual.positional_embedding, 50 x 768) are added; LayerNorm (visual.ln_pre); then 12 pre-LayerNorm residual blocks (visual.transformer.resblocks.0 to .11), each x = x + attention(ln_1(x)) followed by x = x + MLP(ln_2(x)), with 12 attention heads of size 64 and an MLP of width 3,072 with QuickGELU; finally LayerNorm (visual.ln_post) on the class token only and a 768 -> 512 linear projection without bias (visual.proj). The embedding used for retrieval is that 512-dimensional output, L2-normalized. In each block the attention decides which tokens the class token and the patch tokens read from (queries and keys) and what they write back (values and output projection), and the MLP neurons detect features of their input (c_fc rows and biases) and write a fixed direction each (c_proj columns); the residual stream accumulates all these writes, and only the class token at the end becomes the embedding. The exact layout of every weight (for example the row order of the attention matrices: query, key and value rows stacked, each split into 12 heads of 64) is in /model/arch.json. CLIP was trained with a contrastive loss on hundreds of millions of web image-caption pairs: its image embedding is shaped to match the text of a caption. It was never trained to compare one image with another, and in particular never trained to match a sketch with a photo of the same object. The text encoder is part of the release but plays no role in this retrieval.

BACKGROUND: RETRIEVAL AS A DOWNSTREAM TASK. A retrieval embedding is good when, for every query, the relevant gallery items are closer than every irrelevant one. That asks for several things at once, and a network trained for something else can fail at any of them: (i) alignment: the embeddings of a query and of its relevant items must point the same way, whatever else differs between them; (ii) discrimination: items of different categories must be separated along the directions that decide the cosine similarity, not only along some of them; (iii) a well-spread geometry: if all embeddings share a large common component, or if a few high-variance directions dominate the cosine, then similarities are decided by those directions rather than by the object, and some items become "hubs" that rank high for many unrelated queries; (iv) robustness to nuisance factors: whatever varies within a category (drawing style, level of detail, pose, viewpoint, size, position in the frame, background, color, lighting, clutter) should move the embedding little compared with what separates categories. Because the main score is mAP@all, the whole ranking matters: every relevant photo of the category counts wherever it is ranked, and irrelevant photos ranked above relevant ones cost the most when they are at the top. An image-caption model also encodes, besides the object, many things a caption could mention (that an image is a drawing, that it is black and white, its style, its background, text or framing), and these enter the image-image similarity too.

BACKGROUND: THE SKETCH-BASED, ZERO-SHOT SETTING. Sketches and photos differ in almost everything except the object. Here a sketch is thin black strokes on a white background (in these files about 95% of the pixels of a typical sketch are white), drawn in a few seconds by players of an online drawing game from the name of the category rather than from a photo, often very rough and abstract, with no color, no texture, no background scene, simplified or exaggerated shapes, missing parts and an amateur's proportions; a photo is a natural image with color, texture, lighting, clutter and background, in which the object can be small, partial or occluded. So the query and the gallery come from two different visual domains, and the embedding has to bring a sketch close to photos of its category across that gap while keeping categories apart. Typical ways sketch-to-photo retrieval fails: the "drawing versus photograph" information dominates, so a sketch is closer to every other sketch than to any photo; a sketch ranks photos by low-level appearance (an empty or white background, simple outlines, a dominant shape, orientation or size) instead of by object; a sketch is matched to a different category with a similar shape or context; some photos are hubs; some sketches are too abstract or ambiguous; some categories are visually close to each other (fine-grained confusions). Zero-shot means that the evaluation categories are disjoint from every category you can see: an edit that helps only the practice categories (for example by encoding their particular shapes) will not help the evaluation and may hurt it, while an edit that repairs a general cause of bad rankings should carry over. Methods published for this task usually train something on the seen categories (prompt vectors, adapters, the encoder itself, with triplet, contrastive or distillation losses) and apply it to unseen categories; you may not train anything, so you must find the causes in the network and repair them from what you understand.

WHAT MIGHT LIMIT THE RETRIEVAL, AND WHERE. These are possibilities to test, not conclusions; look for others too, and let the measurements decide:
 - Input and early layers: the patch embedding and the first blocks respond to color, texture and fine edges, which sketches lack and photos have in abundance; white or empty patches produce their own strong features; thin strokes may be poorly represented at a 32-pixel patch size.
 - Token mixing: attention heads decide which patches the class token gathers information from; in sketches most patches are blank, and in photos many patches are background; heads that attend to blank or background patches, or to particular positions, can carry appearance rather than object information into the embedding.
 - MLP features: some neurons may fire on "drawing", "white background", "line art", "black and white" or on photographic texture and color, and write directions that differ between domains or dominate the similarity; other neurons may carry object-shape information that is too weak in sketches.
 - The residual stream: a few dimensions or directions with very large values, or a large component shared by all images, can dominate LayerNorm and the final readout.
 - The readout: ln_post and proj decide which directions of the class token reach the embedding and with what weight: directions that separate domains, nuisance factors or hubs, and directions that separate categories, all pass through here.
 - The output geometry: anisotropy, a shared offset, hubs, and the relative weight of domain, category and nuisance directions in the cosine.
 - Category structure: confusions between similar categories, and categories whose sketches are far from their photos.
 The cause can be at one place or spread across depths, and the best place to repair it is not necessarily where it is most visible: a problem visible in the embedding can be cheaper and more general to fix where it is written, or the other way round.

WHAT YOU CAN CHANGE. Any tensor; every one of the following is a legitimate lever if your evidence supports it: the patch embedding filters (visual.conv1.weight), the class token and positional embeddings, the LayerNorm gains and biases (ln_pre, ln_1, ln_2 of every block, ln_post), the attention weights of a head (in_proj_weight and in_proj_bias rows of its query, key or value, which change where it attends or what it reads; out_proj columns, which change what it writes), the MLP neurons (c_fc rows and biases, which change what a neuron detects and when it fires; c_proj columns, which change what it writes), and the final projection (visual.proj). Changes can be sparse (single neurons or heads), low-rank (removing, adding or rotating a few directions in what a component reads or writes), or spread over several layers; they can remove a signal, strengthen one, or change how components respond. Every edit acts on sketches and photos alike, so check its effect on both. Edits are stored in float16 for most matrices (float32 for LayerNorm parameters and embeddings, see DTYPES): changes smaller than float16 resolution vanish.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited encoder and the unedited one are evaluated on a zero-shot split of the QuickDraw dataset: ${n_unseen} object categories, none of which is among your practice categories (no category, sketch or photo of the evaluation is in /practice), about 91,490 sketch queries against a gallery of about 56,410 photos of those categories (from about 1,300 to 3,060 photos per category, median about 1,850, so every sketch must find the photos of its category among those of the other categories). The protocol is fixed: every image is resized to 224x224 (no crop), converted to RGB and normalized with CLIP's mean and standard deviation; the encoder runs in float16 (your float32 edits are cast to each tensor's storage type); the embedding is the encoder output, L2-normalized; every sketch is a query against the whole gallery, ranked by cosine similarity. The main score is mAP@all: for each sketch, over its whole ranking of the gallery, the precision at the position of each relevant photo, averaged over all relevant photos of its category (the average precision), then averaged over all sketches. The second score is P@200: the share of relevant photos among the top 200, averaged over all sketches. mAP@all rewards ranking all relevant photos above the irrelevant ones, especially near the top; P@200 rewards filling the top 200 with relevant photos. Only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the evaluation categories, and their scores are never shown to you.

WHAT YOU SHOULD FIND OUT. Use the practice data to answer questions like these with measurements, and let the answers decide your edits:
 (1) How and why does retrieval fail? Per category and per sketch: which categories retrieve well or badly; which categories the wrongly top-ranked photos come from and what they have in common; whether some photos are hubs; how a sketch's similarity to its own category's photos compares with its similarity to other categories and to other sketches; what distinguishes the sketches that fail from those that succeed.
 (2) What decides the cosine similarity? Which directions of the embedding carry most of its variance, and how much of it is explained by the domain, by the category, by nuisance factors you can measure (from the pixels or the file names), and by nothing you can name; whether the embedding is anisotropic; whether category directions are shared across the two domains.
 (3) Where does each kind of information arise? Follow domain, category and nuisance information through the residual stream (layers 0 to 12), at the class token and at the patch tokens: at which depth each becomes decodable, and whether they live in the same or in different directions.
 (4) Which components write or read it? Attention heads (and which patches they attend to in sketches and in photos), MLP neurons, LayerNorm parameters, the patch embedding and the readout: which of them write the information that hurts the ranking, which carry the information that helps it, and what they respond to in the image.
 (5) What happens when you change them? Show causally that changing a component changes the representation or the retrieval in the predicted way, at several strengths (dose-response), on both domains, without destroying what the retrieval needs (for example, category information among photos and among sketches).
 Then repair the largest causes you can explain, possibly several of them at different places, confirm the result on held-out categories, and stop adding changes when they no longer clearly help.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: a mechanism you have identified (specific neurons, heads, directions, layers or circuits, and what they do on sketches and photos) and a change of the weights that you can justify with evidence. You are not limited to one kind of repair (closing the gap between the domains is one possibility among others: making the network ignore nuisance factors, strengthening the information that separates categories in both domains, changing what attention gathers into the class token, reducing hubs, are others), nor to particular tensors.
